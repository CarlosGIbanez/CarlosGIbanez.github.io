When OpenAI announced ChatGPT in November 2022, few people anticipated the revolution that it would spark. Throughout 2023 new competitors like Claude and Gemini arose, and in 2024 the reasoning-focused models were born. Now, in mid 2026 we have AI agents capable of using software tools, browsing the web, writing and executing code, and acting over long horizons. These agents are building entire apps and systems almost on their own.

You already know all of that, but one question remains: what would happen to my professional career? Would I be able to create substantial value, or will an AI model replace me entirely? In this post, I will try to synthesize the economic research of the top academics in the field. You don't need to be an expert in economics, but some familiarity with common terms is recommended.

My reading of the literature: you need to adapt, the sooner, the better.

## How we dealt with past innovations

Going surprisingly far back in time, we find a very valuable piece of theory in the Industrial Revolution era, with the "Jevons Paradox" (Jevons, 1866). The mechanism described was: when production costs fall (Jevons analyzes the economic effects of technologies that use less coal), output expands, and demand for inputs also increases because you can produce more given the same amount of input. This is one of the reasons that kept the employment level and wages on the rise for centuries, along with a good degree of elasticity in demand.

$$ \text{Costs fall} \to \text{Output expands} \to \text{Input demand rises} $$

Nowadays, we have more sophisticated theories and frameworks to analyze the automation phenomenon.

## Automation replaces tasks, not jobs

The models that command broad consensus (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018; Gans & Goldfarb, 2026; Althoff & Reichardt, 2026) are task-based, not "occupation-based". That is, automating technology doesn't replace an entire job but specific tasks within this job. A job is divided into specific tasks, each aiming to the same objective of producing a good.

One theory that deserves our special attention is _O-Ring_ theory, in which the tasks within a job are _multiplicative_ (_complementary_) rather than additive, and where the time an employee spends in the job is assumed fixed. This has the consequence that both AI and humans can specialize where they offer greater contributions to the production process and neither one completely replaces the other. Workers then specialize in the "bottleneck" activities, that is the activities that the automation technology can't do by itself but it needs in order to do its assigned tasks; and since they enable technology to increase the value of the production, their marginal contribution is higher and so is their wage. This theory is better supported by case-study evidence (Markovic, 2019) than substitution-based theories are.

In general, these frameworks converge to similar conclusions:

1. Automation, even in the rare cases when it can displace entire occupations in the short-run, creates jobs around the tasks technology can't automate.
2. As a consequence, in general equilibrium, these new jobs create more aggregate value, because they deal with bottlenecks so automation can enter the scene
3. There is friction as workers move from obsolete jobs to the new ones, because of the inherent inefficiencies of the labor market, search costs or the time needed to learn new skills and knowledge (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018)

## Empirical findings

So what are we actually observing? Here we find more heterogeneity in the methods and findings of the researchers.

I'm pretty sure you are convinced that generative AI raises the productivity of the one who uses it. But who benefits most? The skilled professional or the lower-skilled? Here the evidence is mixed.

Noy & Zhang (2023) designed an experiment where they gave access to ChatGPT to half of 444 college-educated professionals in a writing task. They found that the productivity gains were superior to the unskilled professionals, so the AI acts as an "equalizer" of abilities. The study of Brynjolfsson et al. (2023), in which they analyzed the use of AI in customer support agents, found similar effects on who benefits more.

On the other hand, Roldan (2025) found that, in the case of academic debates, the use of ChatGPT benefited the skilled and talented students far more than their common peers. And Daniotti et al. (2025), using a deep learning classifier to analyze the code of 170,000 software developers, found that the gains in productivity in this industry were captured almost entirely by senior programmers.

It seems to be that, in complex and long-horizon tasks, the more experienced workers tend to gain more from using the AI than their junior peers. I think this is because architectural and design decisions demand judgment and critical thinking, which remain a bottleneck for AI.

In short:

$$ \text{Short, well specified tasks} \to \text{Equalizing effect} $$

$$ \text{Long-horizon tasks} \to \text{Skill-biased effect} $$

And these findings connect well through the lens of a _complementary-based_ theory, like _O-Ring_.

## So, what would the effect on employment and wages be?

Will AI increase wage inequality and raise unemployment? I find this unlikely. The theoretical models developed by Acemoglu & Restrepo (2018) and Gans & Goldfarb (2026) that have proven robust and consistent with the evidence (Hampole et al., 2025; Acemoglu, 2024) suggest that, even facing strong technologies with high substitution potential, workers are capable of moving to new jobs and specializing where they can produce the greatest value, and this rises equilibrium wages and employment.

It is worth mentioning that the process of adjustment in which the workers specialize in new, more valuable tasks isn't necessarily frictionless or pain-free. As I mentioned earlier, the reallocation of labor is a slow dynamic because of search costs and labor market inefficiencies (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018). And if we take into account the negative effect that the use of AI can have on the very skills that make it valuable (Shen & Tamkin, 2026), the transition may be slower and more painful than the equilibrium models imply.

Now, it is true that employment for juniors and entry-level positions is decreasing (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025), and that AI is widening the gap between skilled and unskilled workers (Roldan, 2025; Daniotti et al., 2025).
This draws a scenario of rough transition dynamics, but this doesn't necessarily imply that juniors are defenseless. As we will see in the next section, most of the abilities possessed by experienced programmers and knowledge workers are complementary to the generative AI (Mäkelä et al., 2026) and, of course, can be learned.

In summary: the models predict long-run adaptation and a rise in wages; but in the short term, they predict (and we are seeing) hard transition dynamics.

## What skills do I need?

You should probably read [this](https://80000hours.org/career-guide/most-valuable-skills/), but I will present some complementary findings here.

Reading the research of Mäkelä et al. (2026), Shen & Tamkin (2026) and the findings of the _World Economic Forum_ (Zahidi, 2025), I found that the most in-demand skills right now are the ones that enable effective and efficient collaboration with AI systems, supervising its behavior, and evaluating and correcting its results; as well as abilities of self-management and collaboration with others, some of these are:

- AI collaboration:
  - Supervising behavior - Can you prevent the AI from taking inefficient or dangerous paths in your job?
  - Evaluation - Can you tell a plausible-looking design from a correct one?
  - Correction of results - Once an answer is produced, can you identify errors fast and reliably?
  - Critical thinking - Is the answer the AI gave you the best it can be?
  - Ethical judgment - Is what you're doing with AI ethical? Does it break any norms?
  - Systems thinking - Are you capable of examining complex systems as a whole?
- Self-management
  - Self-awareness
  - Emotional intelligence
  - Lifelong learning and growth
- Collaboration with others
  - Active listening
  - Communication
  - Conflict resolution
  - Coaching
  - Mentoring

## Now the AGI case

Every model in this post assumes some task remains where humans hold comparative advantage; Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) is precisely the case where that assumption fails.

If AI keeps improving or even enters a self-improving feedback loop, we could soon reach a point where the AI is more intelligent and capable than any human on Earth. What then? If AI reaches near-full automation, labor can lose scarcity and wages can collapse even as output grows, and that will have horrendous consequences: income shifts from labor to capital, and distribution stops being determined by productivity.

I will discuss that possibility in a future post. For now, keep learning and improving yourself!
